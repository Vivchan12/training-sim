When building was expensive, budget forced discipline. Now that anyone can spin up a feature in an afternoon, discipline has to come from somewhere else, and most teams haven't noticed it isn't there anymore.

## The mistake hiding in "it's free"

Someone sees an AI tool draft a module in ten minutes and zero dollars, and concludes the feature is free. It isn't. Drafting is free. Everything after that — integrating it, maintaining it, explaining it to users, fitting it into a system that already has forty other decisions in it — carries a cost that compounds. Interface bloat. Edge cases nobody planned for. Cognitive load nobody budgeted. Architectural debt that doesn't show up on the invoice.

> The tool made creation cheap. It didn't make ownership cheap. Those are two different bills.

## How it plays out, in four stages

This isn't a one-off mistake. It's a cycle, and most teams go through it without naming it:

### 1. Dismissing discovery

The design team asks the basic questions: what job does this do, who's it for, what breaks if we skip it. These get treated as friction, not risk reduction. "No time to explain" becomes the answer to "why does this exist."

### 2. The feature-guzzling binge

With no problem validated, features get bolted on because the tool makes it easy, not because anyone needed it. The team is shipping outputs. Nobody's tracking outcomes.

### 3. The systemic hangover

The user journey starts contradicting itself. Error states multiply. The system gets brittle. Whatever time was saved building fast gets spent firefighting and untangling confused customer feedback.

### 4. Forced enlightenment

Eventually someone rediscovers what customer-development thinking has said for decades, Steve Blank's included: when anyone can build anything by lunchtime, discernment is the only edge left. Building the wrong thing fast is still building the wrong thing. It just got there quicker.

## Why warning people doesn't work

If you're the one who keeps flagging this before it happens, you already know the problem. Repeated warnings don't land as insight. They land as blocking. Say "have we validated this?" enough times and you stop being a strategist and start being the person standing in the way.

The more useful move is often not to keep defending the perimeter. Step back. Quietly document where the system stood before the uncontrolled additions — the baseline, the flows, the drop-off numbers. Then let the consequences do the explaining. A broken journey, a spike in support tickets, users bouncing off a confusing flow: that teaches faster than a design lecture ever does, because it isn't coming from you. It's coming from the thing they built.

## The actual shift

AI didn't remove the need for design thinking. It raised the stakes on it.

Cost used to be the guardrail. Now the guardrail has to be human judgment — knowing what's worth building before you build it, because nothing else is stopping you anymore. Skip that, and "free" turns out to be the most expensive mistake a company can make.
